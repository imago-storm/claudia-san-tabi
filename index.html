Tabi pattern generator

Based on the three-piece tabi pattern in Make Your Own Japanese Clothes by John Marshall (bottom, inpiece, outpiece). See About the source below. Enter your foot tracing measurements in cm. The pieces are drawn at full size: solid line = cut, dashed line = stitch, dash-dot line = fold, dots a to i = the book's matching marks.

How to measure
  1. Trace each foot. This is step 1 in the book. Stand upright on a blank sheet of paper with your full weight on your feet. Ask a friend to trace around each foot with a pencil held straight up (perpendicular to the paper) the whole way round. Trace the left and right foot separately, because feet are often different sizes. Cut them out and label them left and right.
  2. Foot length. On the tracing, find the longest toe (usually the big toe, sometimes the second). Lay a ruler across the tracing touching the back of the heel, and a second one touching the tip of that toe. Both must be square to the long axis of the foot. Measure the distance between them, in cm.
  3. Foot width. Find the widest part of the tracing, which is across the ball of the foot, just behind the toes. Measure straight across it, square to the length line, from edge to edge of the tracing. Don't measure around the foot, only across the tracing.
  4. Compare with the standard shape. Under the width box the page shows how wide the standard bottom piece is at your length. If your tracing is a little narrower or wider, type in your width. If it's about the same, leave the box empty.
  5. Circumference at the top edge. Put a soft tape measure around your leg at the height where the top edge of the tabi will sit. For the standard height that is just above the ankle bone. Keep the tape level with the floor, flat and snug but not tight, and read it in cm. Type it into the circumference box. The page compares it with the opening of the pattern and adjusts the front and top edges for you. Leave it empty to keep the standard opening.
  6. Taller tabi. Stand barefoot on a hard floor. With a ruler or tape, measure straight up the back of your leg from the floor to where you want the top edge to be, in cm, and type it into the height box. The box shows the standard height at the back; if your number is larger, the pieces are made taller by the difference. A leg gets wider higher up, so for a tall tabi also measure the circumference (previous step) at that new height.
  7. Heel and cloth thickness. The heel is under the most strain, so the page adds a little room there (4 mm to start; raise it if your test pair is tight at the heel). If the tabi are lined or the cloth is thick, type the thickness of the outer cloth and of the lining in mm. Each layer wraps round the one inside it, so the page adds extra girth and a bit more heel room automatically. If you also enter the circumference of the top edge, that circumference is treated as the size of your leg and the layers are added on top.
  8. Instep, from a test pair. Sew a test pair from cheap fabric without lining and put it on. Tight across the top of the foot: open the front seam over the instep (the seam where the inpiece and outpiece meet on top of your foot, marked e, f, g on the pieces), put your foot in, bring the two edges together without stretching them, and measure the gap with a ruler in mm. Type half of that gap as a positive number in the instep box. Loose across the top: pinch the extra fabric on top of your foot into one fold, press it flat like a tuck, and measure how deep the fold is in mm, from its base to its crest. Type that depth as a negative number. Half is used because both pieces share the change. Check it with another test pair.

Outline shown is the book's bottom piece, only to show where to measure.

Lengths and circumferences are in cm. Small adjustments and margins are in mm. For length and width use the paper tracing, not a tape around your foot. Tabi are meant to fit snugly (traditionally very snugly), so don't add extra room to your numbers. If you use a stretch fabric, make the tabi a tiny bit smaller than the numbers say. Changing the shape of the toe line isn't supported on this page yet.

DetailValue

Method from the book: the figures are enlarged until the bottom piece is as long as your tracing, and the other pieces by the same amount. The bottom piece is then widened or narrowed to match the tracing (Figs. 16.7 and 16.8). The arch and ankle changes follow Figs. 16.9 to 16.12. The book doesn't give amounts for them, so start with small values (0.3 to 1 cm), sew a muslin test, and adjust.

About the source

Every shape, mark and fitting adjustment on this page comes from one book. Please credit it when you share patterns made here, and if you can, buy or borrow a copy: it has the full sewing instructions that this page leaves out.

Marshall, John. Make Your Own Japanese Clothes: Patterns and Ideas for Modern Wear. Tokyo and New York: Kodansha International, 1988. First edition. ISBN 0-87011-865-X (U.S.), ISBN 4-7700-1365-5 (Japan). Library of Congress catalog number 87-82861.

About the book

John Marshall (born 1955) shows how to make traditional Japanese garments and adapt them for modern wear. The book opens with an introduction to why Japanese clothing is built from narrow straight panels, then covers tools and stitches, making the pattern, and the basic elements (padding, linings, sleeves, hems, collars). The garments are the kimono, outer robe, haori and hanten jackets, wraparound tops, vests, slacks, obi sashes and tabi socks. It ends with chapters on care and storage and appendices on metric conversion, sources of supplies and further reading. There are color photographs on pages 59 to 66. It is dedicated to Mary Tsukamoto.

What this page takes from it

  • Chapter 16, Tabi Socks (pp. 108 to 115). The outlines of the bottom, inpiece and outpiece (Figs. 16.4 to 16.6, pp. 110 to 111), traced from the figures, with the marks a to i.
  • The sizing method (steps 1 to 4, pp. 109 to 111). Trace each foot, enlarge the bottom piece to the length of the tracing, enlarge the other pieces by the same amount, then adjust the width of the bottom (Figs. 16.7 and 16.8).
  • The fit corrections (p. 112). High arch, low arch, slender ankle and thicker ankle (Figs. 16.9 to 16.12). The book shows these as dotted lines without measurements, so the millimeter values on this page are only a way to apply them.
  • The line symbols (p. vi). Solid = cutting line, dashed = sewing line, dash-dot = fold line.
  • Seam allowance (p. 113). "All seam allowances for tabi are 1/4″ unless otherwise indicated." That is 6.4 mm.

What you still need the book for

  • Fabric and materials: about 45 cm (1/2 yard) of 90 cm (36″) wide fabric and the same amount of lining for the inpiece and outpiece, about 25 cm (1/4 yard) of heavy cotton for the bottom, and three kohaze clasps per sock (p. 109).
  • Steps 5 to 16 (pp. 113 to 114): the cutting layout, the sewing order, easing the bottom piece, finishing the edges, and attaching the clasps and thread loops (Figs. 16.13 to 16.26).
  • The variations on p. 115: jika-tabi with rubber soles, tabi shoes, and tall tabi with a front button closing.
